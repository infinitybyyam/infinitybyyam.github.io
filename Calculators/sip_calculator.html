How this is calculated

The method, in plain words

When money goes in. One instalment at the start of every month. Everything is sold at the end of the last month.

How it grows. The fund's yearly cost is taken off the yearly return, and what is left is turned into a true monthly rate, so twelve months of growth add up exactly to that yearly figure. A real fund charges its cost a little every day; the difference from this shortcut is small.

Step-up. The monthly amount goes up by your step-up percentage at the start of each new year.

Tax. Switch tax off to see the result before any tax; switch it back on and your tax values return. Each instalment is checked on its own. If it was held longer than the long-term period, its gain is long-term; otherwise short-term. Long-term gains above the exempt amount are taxed at the long-term rate, short-term gains at the short-term rate, and cess is added on top. Surcharge is not included. The calculator assumes you sell everything in one financial year, so the exempt amount is used once. Selling across several years would use it more than once and lower the tax, so the tax shown here is, if anything, a little high.

Today's money. What reaches your hand is divided by inflation compounded over the full period. It tells you what that money would buy at today's prices.

Currency. Choosing a currency changes only the symbol and how numbers are written. Nothing is converted, so enter every amount in the currency you choose. For rupees the tax settings start with example values; for other currencies they start at zero, because tax depends on where you live. Tax rules change, so always enter the rates that apply to you.

Investing in another currency. When this is switched on, you put money in and get results in your own currency, but the investment grows in the other one. Each year the fund's return after costs is combined with your exchange-rate change: (1 + return) × (1 + yearly change) − 1. If your currency weakens, this adds to your return; if it strengthens, it takes away. Real exchange rates jump around rather than moving by the same amount every year. For rupees, the tax settings switch to example values for shares held abroad, which are often taxed differently from local funds. Enter the rates that apply to you. Fees for converting money and tax collected when money is sent abroad are not included.

Why the common shortcut shows more. A common shortcut takes a 12% yearly return as 1% a month. Compounded, 1% a month is about 12.7% a year, so its answer is higher than the return you typed. Calculators that use it also usually leave out fund costs, tax and inflation.
