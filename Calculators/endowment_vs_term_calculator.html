How this is calculated

The method, in plain words

The idea. Both choices take the same money from you every year. In the policy, the premium buys cover and savings together. In the other choice, part of the same premium buys a term plan for the same cover and the rest goes into a fund. Whenever the policy pays you something, the same amount is taken out of the fund, and once premiums stop, the term premium is paid from the fund. So the only difference is what is left at the end. The headline is that difference in today's money.

When money moves. Premiums, the term premium and money into the fund at the start of each year; payouts and the maturity at the end. Each year runs April to March. Real dates differ a little; the effect is small either way.

Return a year (IRR). The one yearly rate that turns what you paid in into what you got back, after tax. This is the internal rate of return (IRR): it allows for premiums being paid over several years and money coming back at the end. It is not a CAGR worked out from total premiums, which treats everything as paid on day one.

What the policy is sold on. Money back set against premiums paid, as a multiple. It ignores that the money is locked in for years and loses value to inflation, so it always looks better than the return a year.

Bonus. The yearly bonus is your percentage of the cover, added for every year of the term, and paid with the final bonus at maturity. Bonuses are not guaranteed; if they are cut, the policy does worse than shown.

The fund. It grows at your return minus the fund's cost, the same every year, with the cost taken once a year rather than daily. Real returns rise and fall while the policy's guaranteed part does not; the "earns more or less" boxes show how much that matters. If the fund ever cannot pay what it has to, the shortfall counts as extra money from you.

Tax. The policy's maturity and payouts are tax-free while the premium stays within both limits you set; otherwise what you get back above the premiums paid is taxed at your income tax rate when you receive it. The fund's gains are taxed when units are sold, oldest first, at the long-term or short-term rate, with the tax-free amount used once each year. Each year's fund tax is paid at the start of the next by selling units. Losses count only within the same year and are not carried forward, so the fund's tax is, if anything, a little high. No deduction for premiums is given, as under the new tax regime. Surcharge and rebate are left out.

Left out, and which way it pushes. Stopping the policy early usually returns far less than was paid in; the policy is taken as held to maturity, so it looks better here than for anyone who may stop. Death during the term is not compared: both pay the cover, the policy may add bonuses, and the other choice adds whatever is in the fund. A term plan quote that covers more years or a bigger amount than the policy makes the policy look a little better. If you hold other such policies, the yearly premium limit counts them together, so lower it by their premiums.

Today's money. Amounts at the end are divided by inflation over the whole term, so you see what they would buy at today's prices.

Currency. Choosing a currency changes only the symbol and how numbers are written. Nothing is converted, so enter every amount in the currency you choose. For rupees the tax settings start with example values; for other currencies they start at zero, because tax depends on where you live.
