Basics

What is finance?

Meera loved baking sourdough in her flat and sharing loaves with friends, who soon started offering to buy them. As word of mouth spread, juggling orders in a tiny home oven started keeping her up all night. Somewhere around her 40th order, she realized her kitchen was simply too small and began dreaming of her own bakery shop.

The idea costs nothing. The shop does.

So Meera takes a pen and lists what a shop needs. An oven. A month's rent. Flour. Someone at the counter while she bakes. The oven alone is ₹8 lakh, and rent, flour and wages for the first months need another ₹2 lakh. She has ₹4 lakh.

The idea cost her nothing, but opening the shop leaves a ₹6 lakh gap. Before she can even open the doors, three core financial choices stand right in front of her.

Giving the worry a name

Those three questions together, where the money comes from, where it goes and what comes back, are what finance is. It isn't only about bakeries. Meera's parents deciding whether to prepay their home loan are asking the same kind of question.

As Meera's bakery will transform from a kitchen hobby into a corporation, finance concepts will scale up alongside her. People use different terms for different slices of this machine. Tap a layer below to see how Meera’s questions evolve across different layers of finance.

Cutting across

Now let us follow Meera's growth step by step. She begins with her first major hurdle: choosing between bank debt and a partner to fund that ₹10 lakh shop.

Funding the shop (Financing Decision)

To open the ₹10 lakh shop, Meera puts in her ₹4 lakh savings, leaving ₹6 lakh to be raised. She has two options: take a bank loan at 12% annual interest, or bring in a partner who takes an equity share in exchange for cash.

The bank demands its interest payment, rain or shine. An equity partner absorbs risk alongside her but claims a permanent cut of all future upside.

Who funds the shop?

The shop needs ₹10 lakh. Meera puts in ₹4 lakh. The other ₹6 lakh comes from the bank, a partner, or a mix. The shop is valued at ₹10 lakh, so every ₹1 lakh a partner puts in buys him 10% of it. In a normal year the shop earns ₹1.5 lakh before any financing cost, a 15% return. In a slow year it earns ₹0.6 lakh, a 6% return.

All figures in ₹ lakh per year. Every rupee borrowed instead of taken from a partner lets Meera keep more of the shop, but the bank is paid first. In a normal year that lifts her share until about ₹4 lakh of borrowing, after which the extra interest outweighs it. In a slow year more borrowing soon works against her, and at ₹6 lakh she is below zero. That trade is called leverage.

Borrowing introduces fixed obligations that make slow years harder. Once funding is secured and the bakery is open and thriving, Meera faces her next big hurdle: deciding where to reinvest her extra cash to grow.

Allocating capital (Investment Decision)

With her bakery performing well, over a few years Meera accumulates ₹10 lakh from the profit she keeps and evaluates two growth options. Plan A adds a second counter generating a steady ₹3 lakh per year for 5 years (₹15 lakh total). Plan B launches a corporate catering deal that yields ₹0 in years 1 and 2, then ₹2 lakh, ₹6 lakh, and ₹8 lakh in years 3 to 5 (₹16 lakh total).

Accounting profit suggests Plan B is superior because ₹16 lakh is bigger than ₹15 lakh. However, finance accounts for timing and risk; money received sooner can be reinvested immediately. Let's test to see what each plan is actually worth today.

Looking strictly at accounting profit fails because it ignores cash timing, opportunity cost, and risk. Sound investment decisions target wealth creation: discounting all future net cash flows back to their value today. If her local shop ever grew large enough to list publicly one day, its share price would simply reflect the market's running estimate of that exact wealth.

Sharing the spoils (Dividend Decision)

Once investments start generating surplus earnings, Meera reaches her third master choice: The Dividend Decision.

She must decide how to split the profits. Should she pay out that cash to herself and her equity partner as a cash dividend, or retain the profits inside the company to fund a brand new second branch? If she brought in an equity partner earlier, this will no longer be just her solo call.

Paying dividends gives owners immediate cash rewards to use as they please. Retaining earnings feeds future organic business growth without taking on extra bank debt. Striking the right balance here determines how fast the bakery expands versus how happy the current owners are.

Meera has figured out how to fund the shop, where to grow, and how to split the profits. But she is about to learn a brutal lesson: paper profit and cold hard cash are two entirely different worlds.

Daily reality (Managing Cash Flow)

Accounting records a sale the day it is made. Finance asks a harder question: when does the money land in the bank? To scale up further, Meera starts supplying sourdough loaves in bulk to local cafes.

Her counter still sells ₹2.5 lakh a month, paid on the spot. The cafés add ₹1 lakh a month, and flour, staff wages, packing and rent now cost ₹3.2 lakh. On paper, her monthly profit is ₹0.3 lakh. Growing the bakery has used up most of her savings, so she starts the month with just ₹0.5 lakh in the bank. And the cafes insist on paying on 30-day credit terms.

Month one, after the cafés pay

This is month one only. Counter customers pay on the spot. Once cafés take 30 days to pay, none of this month’s café sales has reached the bank yet.

Meera cannot pay her landlord or flour supplier with paper profit; she can only pay with cash. Managing working capital gaps, maintaining liquidity, and aligning capital decisions is the primary domain of the Chief Financial Officer (CFO). Today, the CFO works directly alongside the CEO to ensure that aggressive growth is supported by cash solvency.

Scaling up (The Agency Problem)

As the bakery grows to two shops, Meera can no longer manage daily operations at both. She hires a professional general manager to run the day-to-day business.

This introduces a fundamental corporate conflict: the Agency Problem. As an owner, Meera wants long-term wealth creation. The manager, however, may prioritize expanding head-count and opening prestige stores to boost his own resume, even if those decisions lower total profits. The fix is alignment: tying a manager's rewards to the actual value he builds changes the projects he decides to take on.

Aligning manager incentives with long-term wealth creation reduces agency costs. Lenders face similar agency risks, enforcing strict debt covenants to protect their capital. From a neighborhood sourdough bakery to a multi-billion dollar enterprise, the core principles of finance remain identical: raising capital, making sound investments, deciding payouts, managing cash flow, and aligning corporate control.
