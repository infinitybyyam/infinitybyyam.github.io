Basics

Is the shop actually healthy?

Years fly by in a blur of flour, early dawns, and relentless hard work. Suddenly, Meera’s bakery is five years old. The last loaf of Year 5 is cooling on the rack, and the bakery is finally quiet. By every metric, it was her best year yet: more customers, cafés across town ordering her sourdough, and more profit than ever. Then she sits down with her numbers. Number crunching is never easy, and looking at them, she might actually need to start eating some of her own sourdough just to get by.

How she got here

When Meera opened five years ago with ₹10 lakh; part her savings, part her partner’s stake, and part a bank loan; every single loaf felt like a high-stakes gamble. Today, the shelves move fast, the ovens hum in a steady rhythm, and annual sales have climbed past forty lakhs.

But growth has a way of altering the landscape. To keep up with demand from local cafés, Meera dropped ten lakhs from her saved cash reserves at the start of Year 5 to install a second oven and a bigger kitchen. Paying for growth out of profit the shop has already kept is called funding from retained earnings.

Profit up, cash down. Both are true, and her books can explain how. But first Meera needs to know what she is looking at.

The film and the photograph (Financial Statements)

Everything Meera needs sits on two sheets of paper. The income statement is a film of the whole year: what came in from sales, what went out in costs, and what was left as profit. The balance sheet is a photograph of the last evening of the year: what the shop owns, what it owes, and what belongs to the owners.

Meera already learned in her first month that profit is not cash sitting in a bank account. Because earnings on paper do not always match physical currency, a "third view" is needed to walk step-by-step from the year's net profit down to the actual cash left in the bank.

Now look at that ₹3.51 lakh of profit. On its own, the number doesn't tell you much. It’s a lot for a corner shop, but tiny for a factory. When you measure it against the ₹42 lakh of sales it took to make it, the picture clears up: the shop keeps about ₹8 of every ₹100 it sells. That comparison is a ratio; a simple benchmark you can hold up against last year, or against the bakery down the road.

Four visitors, four questions (Users of Financial Statements)

That week the books draw a small crowd. Her flour supplier wants to know whether his bills will be paid on time. The bank manager wants to know whether to lend again. Her partner wants to know what his 30% is earning, and why none of it has reached him. And Meera wants to know where to look first.

Each question has its own family of ratios: profitability for what the shop earns, activity for how long money stays stuck, liquidity for paying the bills due soon, and leverage for whose money is in the shop.

Did the shop earn well? (Profitability Ratios)

Her partner's question comes first: did the shop actually earn well? To answer it, Meera takes ₹100 that came in at the counter and follows it down the income statement, cost by cost, to see what is left at each step. That gives her three margins: how much of each sale the shop keeps.

Then she asks a second question, about the money rather than the sales: how hard is the money in the shop working? That gives her three returns.

The shop earns well: every ₹100 in it earns about ₹23 a year, nearly twice what the bank charges. But each sale keeps less than it did last year. The cafés brought volume at a thinner margin, and they brought something else too: a wait.

Where is the money stuck? (Activity Ratios)

A counter customer pays before the loaf leaves the shop. A café takes the loaf today and pays a month later. Meanwhile flour waits on the shelf for about two weeks, and since the cafés arrived Meera has started taking a month to pay her own flour supplier instead of two weeks.

Activity ratios count this waiting in days: stock days, receivable days and payable days. Add the days flour sits on the shelf to the days the cafés take to pay, take away the days the supplier waits, and you have the stretch Meera has to fund herself. That stretch is the cash gap.

This year the gap held up ₹0.4 lakh of her money. Had the supplier not waited longer, it would have been nearly ₹1 lakh. That favour works only while the supplier is happy, and he is exactly the one asking.

Can she pay the bills due soon? (Liquidity Ratios)

The supplier's question is about the next few months, not the next few years. Will what comes in soon cover what goes out soon? The current ratio sets cash, money owed by cafés and stock against the bills due within the year. The quick ratio leaves out stock, because half a bag of flour cannot pay a bill. The cash ratio keeps only the cash.

Bills due within the year include the supplier and any part of a bank loan to be repaid next year. Meera paid her last instalment at the end of Year 5, so only the supplier is left. You will often hear 2 and 1 quoted as safe levels. Treat them as habits, not laws: a café paid at the counter can live below 1, while a wholesaler with slow customers can struggle at 2.

Whose money is in the shop? (Leverage Ratios)

Everything the shop owns was paid for by someone: the owners, the bank or the supplier. Debt to equity compares what the shop has borrowed with what the owners have put in and kept. Debt to assets shows the bank's slice of the whole. The thinner the owners' slice, the less room there is for a bad year.

The bank also asks whether the shop can carry its loan. Interest cover is profit before interest divided by interest: at 4, profit could fall to a quarter before the interest went unpaid. Loan-payment cover sets what the shop earns before wear and interest against the whole instalment, because the bank wants its money back as well as its interest.

Meera's own answer is simple: the loan is gone, and the bank manager would happily lend again. Whether she should borrow is a question for later. First she lays the years side by side.

Across the years, down the page (Horizontal and Vertical Analysis)

Two simple habits make a set of books easier to read. Horizontal analysis puts this year beside last year and asks how much each line changed. Vertical analysis turns every line into a share of sales, or of everything the shop owns.

Shares strip out size, so Meera's small Year 1 can sit beside her busy Year 5, or beside a bigger bakery. And any line growing faster than sales deserves a question.

Ingredients, wages and wear all outran sales this year. None of them is alarming alone, but together they explain why a 40% jump in sales became an 8% rise in profit.

Clues, not verdicts (Limits of Ratios)

Ratios are quick, but they are built from accounting figures with blind spots. A balance sheet is a single evening, and a big purchase or a late payment can make the same shop look very different a day apart.

Assets sit in the books at what they cost, less wear, so Meera's first oven now looks worth ₹4 lakh though it bakes as well as ever. Two shops can make different accounting choices and report different profits for the same reality. And no ratio has one right level.

What Meera found

Back at the counter, Meera puts it all together. The shop earns well: the money in it returns about 23%, far above what the bank charged. But profit rose only 8% while sales rose 40%. Café loaves sell cheaper, the cafés needed more staff, and the new kitchen wears out too.

Her bills are safe, covered almost five times, and the bank loan is paid off. What changed is the waiting. The cafés hold a month of their sales, she holds her supplier's money for a month, and ₹10 lakh of savings became an oven and a kitchen.

The shop is healthy. It is also using more of its own cash to grow.

That is all a health check gives: not a grade, but a place to look. And Meera is already looking at the next thing, a second shop. It will need more money than the till holds, and how she raises it will change how risky the shop becomes.
